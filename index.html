Course Projects

I am interested in how circuits in the auditory system process sound, and I build mechanistic models to study them. This page collects three course projects.

Circuit Model of Stimulus-Specific Adaptation in Auditory Cortex

MATH 568, Mathematical and Computational Neuroscience · Sept 2026 – present · In progress

Neurons in auditory cortex respond less to a repeated tone and more to a rare one. This project asks how much of that tone-by-tone adaptation comes from depression at thalamocortical synapses, and how much from short-term plasticity at interneuron synapses.

I add activity-dependent Tsodyks–Markram short-term plasticity to the synapses to and from parvalbumin (PV) and somatostatin (SST) interneurons in the Park and Geffen (2020) circuit model. I fit the original and extended models to oddball recordings from 114 neurons in rat auditory cortex (Vázquez-Borsetti et al., 2026) and test how well each predicts held-out responses after a deviant tone.

I derived a closed-form tone-to-tone map for thalamocortical depression. It predicts synaptic strength at each tone onset from the model parameters and agrees with simulation to within 0.003.

Cochlear Implant Speech Processing and Intelligibility

EECS 351, Digital Signal Processing · Sept 2026 – present · In progress

A cochlear implant reduces speech to slow amplitude envelopes in a small number of frequency channels. This project builds that processing chain in Python and measures how much intelligibility survives it.

The pipeline uses a Greenwood-spaced band-pass filter bank, envelope extraction by full-wave rectification and low-pass filtering, and power-law compression, followed by noise-vocoder resynthesis. I vary channel count from 1 to 22, envelope cutoff, and compression level, and score the output with short-time objective intelligibility (STOI) and the word error rate (WER) of an automatic speech recognizer.

The two measures disagree. STOI scores of 0.47 to 0.64 occur alongside WER of 100% or more. I am extending the framework to speech in background noise to test whether front-end denoising helps.

Thalamocortical Network Model of Absence Seizures

MATH 462, Mathematical Models, team project · Jan – Apr 2025 · Completed

Absence seizures appear as 3 Hz discharges that involve both cortex and thalamus. This project models how feedback from cortex to thalamus can move a network from normal gamma oscillations into that seizure-like rhythm.

We extended a conductance-based pyramidal–interneuron network gamma (PING) model to a 350-neuron thalamocortical network with relay and reticular populations. The model includes T-type calcium currents, AMPA, GABAA, and GABAB synapses, and corticothalamic feedback.

The network shifts from gamma oscillations to 3 Hz seizure-like discharges depending on corticothalamic feedback, which we characterized with spectral, bursting, and synchrony analyses. A fast–slow bifurcation analysis identifies a saddle-node threshold for thalamic rebound bursting and explains how slow GABAB inhibition sets the discharge period.
